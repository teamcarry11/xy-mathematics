## Executive Summary

From a Modern Monetary Theory (MMT) perspective, California's high taxes on working-class people and legal immigrants are economically counterproductive. While California uses a progressive income tax structure, the combination of income taxes and regressive sales taxes creates a significant burden that reduces effective demand without effectively managing inflation. Nevada's no-income-tax model, while still relying on regressive sales taxes, leaves more money in the hands of working-class people, supporting stronger economic participation and integration.

**Key Insight**: MMT teaches that taxes don't "fund" government spending for sovereign currency issuers. Instead, taxes serve to create demand for currency, manage inflation, redistribute resources, and influence behavior. From this perspective, taxing working-class people heavily is counterproductive because it reduces effective demand (they spend all their income) without effectively controlling inflation (which comes from real resource constraints, not just money supply).

## The Tax Difference: California vs Nevada

### California Tax Structure (2025)

**State Income Tax** (Progressive):
- 1% on income up to $10,756
- 2% on income $10,757 - $25,499
- 4% on income $25,500 - $40,245
- 6% on income $40,246 - $55,866
- 8% on income $55,867 - $70,606
- 9.3% on income $70,607 - $360,659
- 10.3% on income $360,660 - $432,787
- 11.3% on income $432,788 - $721,314
- 12.3% on income over $721,314
- Plus 1% surcharge on income over $1 million (top rate: 13.3%)

**Example**: For a working-class individual earning $50,000 annually:
- Total state income tax: approximately **$1,500**

**Sales Tax** (Regressive):
- Combined state and local rates: ~7.5% average
- **Regressive impact**: Lowest 20% of income earners pay ~7.4% of their income in sales/excise taxes
- Top 1% pay ~0.8% of their income in sales/excise taxes
- In Bay Area: Households earning <$30,000 pay nearly 6% of total income on sales taxes for necessities

**Refundable Credits** (Partial Relief):
- California Earned Income Tax Credit (CalEITC)
- Young Child Tax Credit (YCTC) — maximum $1,154 per family (2024)
- Helpful but limited in scope

### Nevada Tax Structure (2025)

**State Income Tax**: **None**
- No state tax on wages, salaries, capital gains, or retirement distributions

**Sales Tax**:
- Combined state and local rates: ~6.85% average
- Still regressive, but no income tax burden

**Example**: For a working-class individual earning $50,000 annually:
- State income tax: **$0**
- **Savings vs California**: approximately $1,500 annually

## Modern Monetary Theory (MMT) Perspective on Taxation

### What MMT Says About Taxes

According to MMT economists like **L. Randall Wray** and **Stephanie Kelton**, taxes serve four primary functions in a sovereign currency system:

1. **Driving Demand for the Currency**: By requiring taxes to be paid in the government's currency, the state creates demand for that currency, establishing its value and acceptance.

2. **Controlling Inflation**: Taxes help regulate aggregate demand. Higher taxes reduce spending power (preventing inflation), while lower taxes increase spending power (stimulating activity when needed).

3. **Addressing Income Inequality**: Progressive taxation redistributes wealth, reducing income disparities and promoting social equity.

4. **Influencing Behavior**: Taxes can encourage or discourage specific behaviors (e.g., carbon taxes, sin taxes, investment incentives).

**Critical MMT Insight**: Taxes do **not** primarily fund government expenditures. Since a sovereign government can issue its own currency, it doesn't rely on tax revenues to finance spending. Instead, taxes are tools for managing economic stability, controlling inflation, and achieving social objectives.

## The Problem with High Working-Class Taxes (MMT Analysis)

### 1. Reduces Effective Demand

**The Issue**: Working-class people spend nearly all of their income on necessities (housing, food, transportation, healthcare). When you tax their income, you're directly reducing their spending power.

**MMT Perspective**: This is economically counterproductive. Working-class spending drives the economy through consumption. Taking money from people who spend 100% of their income reduces aggregate demand, potentially weakening economic growth.

**California Impact**: A $50k earner pays ~$1,500 in state income tax plus ~$3,750 in sales taxes (7.5% of $50k) = **$5,250 total tax burden** (10.5% of income). This significantly reduces their spending power.

**Nevada Impact**: A $50k earner pays ~$3,425 in sales taxes (6.85% of $50k) = **$3,425 total tax burden** (6.85% of income). More money remains in their hands for spending.

### 2. Inflation Control Misses the Point

**The Issue**: Conventional thinking suggests taxing working-class people helps control inflation by reducing money supply.

**MMT Perspective**: Inflation comes from **real resource constraints** (labor shortages, supply chain bottlenecks, material scarcity), not just money supply. Taxing people who spend all their income doesn't address the root causes of inflation.

**Better Approach**: To control inflation, tax those with **high savings rates** (high earners, wealth holders) who aren't spending their money anyway. This reduces aggregate demand without hurting working-class spending.

### 3. Regressive Sales Tax Compounds the Problem

**The Issue**: California's sales tax is highly regressive:
- Lowest 20% of earners: ~7.4% of income in sales/excise taxes
- Top 1% of earners: ~0.8% of income in sales/excise taxes

**MMT Perspective**: This creates a double burden on working-class people:
- Progressive income tax (good in theory)
- Regressive sales tax (bad in practice)
- **Net effect**: Still heavily burdens working-class people

**California's Combined Burden**: Even with progressive income tax, the regressive sales tax means working-class people pay a higher effective tax rate than the system appears to suggest.

### 4. Impact on Legal Immigrants

**The Issue**: Legal immigrants often:
- Start with lower incomes while building economic security
- Send remittances to family abroad
- Face additional costs (visa fees, language barriers, credential recognition)
- Need to build savings for stability

**MMT Perspective**: High taxes on immigrants:
- Reduce their ability to integrate economically
- Limit their spending power (hurting local economies)
- Make it harder to build financial security
- Don't effectively manage inflation (they spend all their income anyway)

**Nevada Advantage**: No income tax means immigrants keep more of their earnings, supporting:
- Faster economic integration
- Stronger local spending (supporting businesses)
- Better ability to build savings and stability
- Remittances that support global economic connections

## What MMT Would Suggest

### Optimal Tax Policy (MMT-Aligned)

1. **Shift Tax Burden Away from Working-Class**:
   - Eliminate or dramatically reduce income taxes for working-class people
   - Target high earners and wealth holders instead (they have high savings rates)

2. **Fix Regressive Sales Taxes**:
   - Exempt necessities (food, medicine, basic clothing) from sales tax
   - Or implement progressive sales tax credits/rebates for low-income households
   - Focus sales taxes on luxury goods and services

3. **Use Taxes to Manage Inflation Effectively**:
   - Tax those with high savings rates (they're not spending anyway)
   - Don't tax those who spend all their income (they drive the economy)
   - Address real resource constraints (labor, materials, supply chains) directly

4. **Support Immigrant Economic Integration**:
   - Lower taxes on working-class immigrants (they spend all their income)
   - Support their spending power (drives local economies)
   - Enable faster financial security building

### California's Current Approach: Mixed Results

**What's Good**:
- ✅ Progressive income tax structure
- ✅ Refundable tax credits (CalEITC, YCTC)
- ✅ Higher taxes on high earners

**What's Problematic**:
- ❌ Regressive sales tax offsets progressive income tax benefits
- ❌ Still taxes working-class people significantly
- ❌ Reduces effective demand from working-class spending
- ❌ Doesn't effectively manage inflation (targets wrong group)

**Net Effect**: California's system is better than a flat tax, but still burdens working-class people more than MMT would suggest is optimal.

### Nevada's Approach: Better for Working-Class, But...

**What's Good**:
- ✅ No income tax (leaves more money in working-class hands)
- ✅ Better for working-class people and immigrants
- ✅ Supports higher effective demand
- ✅ Faster economic integration for immigrants

**What's Problematic**:
- ❌ Still relies on regressive sales tax
- ❌ Revenue volatility (tourism-dependent)
- ❌ Less progressive redistribution

**Net Effect**: Nevada's no-income-tax model is better for working-class people from an MMT perspective, but still relies on regressive sales taxes.

## The Bigger Picture: MMT and State Tax Policy

### Key MMT Principles Applied to State Taxes

**1. Taxes Don't Fund Spending**:
- States can't issue currency (only federal government can)
- But the principle still applies: taxes are tools for managing the economy, not just revenue collection

**2. Effective Demand Matters**:
- Working-class people spend all their income → they drive the economy
- High earners save most of their income → they don't drive consumption
- Tax policy should reflect this reality

**3. Inflation Control Requires Targeting**:
- Tax those with high savings rates (they're not spending anyway)
- Don't tax those who spend all their income (they're not causing inflation)
- Address real resource constraints directly

**4. Redistribution is a Tool**:
- Progressive taxation redistributes resources
- But regressive sales taxes undermine this
- Net effect matters more than individual tax types

### Policy Implications

**For California**:
- Consider eliminating or reducing income taxes for working-class people
- Exempt necessities from sales tax
- Increase taxes on high earners/wealth holders
- Use taxes to manage inflation by targeting savings, not spending

**For Nevada**:
- Current no-income-tax model is better for working-class people
- But should consider exempting necessities from sales tax
- Could add progressive income tax on high earners (without taxing working-class)
- Maintain focus on supporting working-class spending power

**For Working-Class People and Immigrants**:
- Nevada's model is more favorable (no income tax)
- But both states rely on regressive sales taxes
- Ideal system: No income tax on working-class + sales tax exemptions for necessities

## Conclusion

From a Modern Monetary Theory perspective, California's high taxes on working-class people and legal immigrants are economically counterproductive. While California uses a progressive income tax structure, the combination of income taxes and regressive sales taxes creates a significant burden that:

1. **Reduces effective demand** (working-class people spend all their income)
2. **Doesn't effectively manage inflation** (inflation comes from resource constraints, not working-class spending)
3. **Undermines redistribution** (regressive sales tax offsets progressive income tax)
4. **Hinders immigrant integration** (reduces spending power and savings ability)

Nevada's no-income-tax model is better for working-class people and immigrants from an MMT perspective, as it:
- Leaves more money in working-class hands (supporting effective demand)
- Enables faster economic integration for immigrants
- Supports stronger local spending (driving economic growth)

However, both states still rely on regressive sales taxes, which burden working-class people disproportionately.

**The MMT-Aligned Solution**: Shift tax burden away from working-class people entirely (eliminate income taxes for them), exempt necessities from sales tax, and target high earners/wealth holders (who have high savings rates) for inflation management and redistribution.
